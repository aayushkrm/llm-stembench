## Scope

LLM-STEMBench evaluates large language models on MMLU-STEM with accuracy, calibration, and statistically principled model comparison; it constructs a taxonomy of mathematical and scientific reasoning errors; and it introduces an original bilingual Russian–English benchmark spanning mathematics, physics, and chemistry with a paired-item design. This review synthesizes the literature that underwrites each of those design decisions, and closes by identifying the gap the project fills.

## 1. Benchmarks for exact-science evaluation

Multiple-choice knowledge benchmarks established the modern template for evaluating what LLMs know. MMLU [Hendrycks2021a] covers 57 subjects, including the mathematics, physics, and chemistry slices our project uses, and demonstrated that broad exact-science competence can be measured with a single, automatically scored instrument. Its limitations are equally instructive: four-option items are susceptible to guessing and option-position bias, and scores move several points with trivial prompt changes. MMLU-Pro [Wang2024] addresses both problems — ten options instead of four, harder reasoning-oriented items, noisy items removed — and reports 16–33% lower accuracy along with roughly halved prompt sensitivity; it is the methodological reference for how we report the robustness of our own benchmark scores across prompt templates.

For free-form exact-science answers, MATH [Hendrycks2021b] and GSM8K [Cobbe2021] define the conventions we adopt: problems with a unique checkable final answer, normalization rules for equivalence checking, and a corpus large enough for per-difficulty reporting. GSM8K additionally motivates our error-analysis workstream: its authors' central finding was that final-answer accuracy conceals whether the model reasoned correctly or guessed. Science-specific evaluation is systematized by SciEval [Sun2024], which organizes items by cognitive level in the spirit of Bloom's taxonomy and includes a dynamic subset designed to resist data leakage; both the level structure and the dynamic-subset idea transfer directly to our item design. Two truthfulness-focused benchmarks bracket the accuracy-only view: TruthfulQA [Lin2022] shows models can be systematically and confidently wrong in predictable categories, and HaluEval [Li2023] demonstrates a workable pipeline for harvesting real hallucinations from model outputs — the same bottom-up procedure we use to seed error-taxonomy categories with observed failures.

## 2. Validity threats: data contamination

Benchmark scores are only meaningful if the test data was unseen during training. Sainz et al. [Sainz2023] argue that web-scale pretraining has made contamination the default assumption rather than the exception, propose graded contamination levels, and call for benchmark-specific detection to be reported alongside every evaluation result. Golchin and Surdeanu [Golchin2024] supply a practical probe: prompt the model with the dataset name, split, and the initial segment of an instance, then score the completion's overlap with the reference — achieving 92–100% agreement with human expert judgments. Oren et al. [Oren2023] contribute the statistical framing we find most compelling: because an uncontaminated model should find every ordering of an exchangeable benchmark equally likely, a measurable preference for canonical orderings is provable evidence of contamination, detectable even with ~1000-item test sets. Together these three papers dictate our mitigation strategy: a small public sample with the paired-item test set held out [Sainz2023], completion- and ordering-based leakage probes before release [Golchin2024; Oren2023], and template-generated variants so that every released item instantiates an unseen template (see §7).

## 3. Measurement mechanics: answer extraction and format sensitivity

A recurring threat to internal validity is that measured "accuracy" partially measures the harness. Zheng et al. [Zheng2024] show that LLMs exhibit selection bias — systematic preference for particular option IDs rooted in token-level probability mass — so reordering options changes MMLU-style scores; their label-free PriDe debiasing estimates the option-ID prior from a few permuted samples. Our MMLU-STEM pipeline therefore permutes options and reports extraction robustness rather than trusting a single letter-parsing run. Output format is a second confound: Tam et al. [Tam2024] find that strict structured-output constraints (JSON, schemas) measurably degrade reasoning, and He et al. [He2024] find that merely rendering the same prompt as plain text, Markdown, JSON, or YAML shifts GPT-3.5-turbo performance by up to 40% on some tasks. Our harness consequently fixes one documented prompt template per language, uses a tolerant answer grammar with logged extraction failures, and treats format sensitivity as a reported experimental condition rather than noise.

## 4. Calibration

Accuracy says nothing about whether model confidence is usable. Guo et al. [Guo2017] established the standard diagnostics — reliability diagrams and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) computed over binned confidence intervals — and showed that modern networks are reliably overconfident, with temperature scaling as an effective single-parameter remedy. We adopt their binned-ECE definition for both MMLU-STEM and the bilingual benchmark. Because ECE estimates are sensitive to the choice of bins — a real concern at our per-subject sample sizes — we follow Naeini et al. [Naeini2015], whose Bayesian Binning into Quantiles averages calibration estimates over many binnings, as the robustness check on our reported ECE values.

## 5. Statistical comparison of models

Our comparison methodology deliberately favors matched-item designs, where every model answers the same questions. For interval estimates of per-subject accuracy we use Wilson score intervals [Wilson1927], whose asymmetric bounds retain nominal coverage at the small, skewed sample sizes typical of MMLU-STEM subject slices, where Wald intervals fail. For statistics with no convenient closed form (ECE, aggregate scores, cross-language gaps) we bootstrap over items [Efron1979]. Paired model comparison on shared items with binary outcomes is exactly McNemar's setting; Dietterich's comparison of candidate tests recommends McNemar's test for two algorithms on a single test set and warns against resampled-t variants whose independence assumptions inflate Type I error [Dietterich1998]. Extending beyond two models, Cochran's Q tests the null of equal performance across k models on matched binary outcomes, with a chi-squared reference distribution on k−1 degrees of freedom [Cochran1950] — our omnibus test before pairwise McNemar comparisons. Because a single experiment generates dozens of p-values (model pairs × subjects × languages), we apply the Benjamini–Hochberg step-up procedure, which controls the false discovery rate under independence or positive dependence and is markedly more powerful than Bonferroni-style family-wise control [Benjamini1995].

## 6. Error taxonomies for reasoning failures

The error-taxonomy workstream rests on three results. Lightman et al. [Lightman2023] show that process supervision — judging each reasoning step rather than only the final answer — substantially outperforms outcome supervision on MATH and release PRM800K, 800K step-level human labels; this validates our choice to code the first faulty step, not merely whether the item failed. Tyen et al. [Tyen2024] demonstrate that LLMs are poor at detecting reasoning errors even though they can correct errors once shown the location — a decisive argument for human coders (with measured agreement) over LLM self-critique in our annotation protocol, and the source of our "undetected prior-step error" category. Mirzadeh et al. [Mirzadeh2024] show that GSM8K performance is brittle to irrelevant perturbations (adding clauses can cost up to 65% accuracy), and hypothesize that models replicate memorized reasoning patterns rather than reason; this motivates both a taxonomy category for pattern-matching failures and our use of controlled item variants as diagnostic items.

## 7. Multilingual and bilingual evaluation

Multilingual evaluation has largely proceeded by translating an established monolingual benchmark. MGSM [Shi2022] translated 250 GSM8K problems into ten typologically diverse languages and found that chain-of-thought ability transfers across languages, emerging with scale — establishing translated paired items as a valid instrument for separating language effects from reasoning effects. For Russian specifically, MERA [Fenogenova2024] provides 21 generative tasks with held-out answer keys and a unified evaluation methodology, and documents that open models remain far below human level in Russian. LLM-STEMBench sits precisely at the intersection of these two lines: MGSM-style paired items (identical content, two languages), extended beyond arithmetic word problems into physics and chemistry, with MERA-style leakage protection. Unlike MGSM, we analyze per-item language effects on matched content — which RU/EN item pairs flip correctness is itself taxonomy evidence — and unlike MERA, the pairing makes cross-language comparison a paired statistical test rather than a leaderboard juxtaposition.

## 8. Annotation reliability

Because the error taxonomy is produced by human coders, its credibility depends on reported inter-annotator agreement. Cohen's kappa corrects raw percent agreement for chance for exactly two raters [Cohen1960]; Fleiss' generalization handles any number of raters per item under a rater-exchangeability assumption [Fleiss1971]. Krippendorff's alpha dominates both in flexibility — any number of coders, missing data, any measurement level, bootstrap confidence intervals — and is the standard our protocol adopts as primary, following [Hayes2007] and the canonical treatment in [Krippendorff2013], with the conventional α ≥ 0.8 threshold for firm conclusions and 0.667–0.8 for tentative ones. Prevalence effects on kappa mean we report per-category base rates alongside agreement coefficients [Cohen1960; Krippendorff2013].

## 9. Safety relevance and documentation practice

Finally, we treat rigorous STEM capability measurement as safety-relevant work. Hendrycks et al. [Hendrycks2021c] established evaluation datasets as instruments for steering AI toward shared human values, and Shevlane et al. [Shevlane2023] frame systematic capability evaluation as the evidence base for training, deployment, and disclosure decisions about dangerous capabilities — implying that honest, uncertainty-quantified STEM measurements (not inflated, contaminated, or unparsed-caveat leaderboards) are a governance input. Documentation practice follows the same logic: model cards disaggregate performance and state intended uses [Mitchell2019], and datasheets force dataset creators to disclose motivation, composition, collection, and maintenance before release [Gebru2021]. Our deliverables adopt both formats: per-model result cards with disaggregated, interval-estimated scores, and a datasheet-style benchmark card for the released bilingual item pool.

## 10. Synthesis and gap

Read together, the literature makes four demands on any new evaluation study: measure beyond raw accuracy (calibration [Guo2017], error structure [Lightman2023]); quantify uncertainty in every comparison (intervals and paired tests [Wilson1927; Dietterich1998; Cochran1950; Benjamini1995]); control the harness itself (extraction bias [Zheng2024], format sensitivity [Tam2024; He2024]); and protect validity at the data layer (contamination [Sainz2023; Golchin2024; Oren2023]). No existing benchmark, to our knowledge, combines all of the following: bilingual Russian–English paired items across mathematics, physics, and chemistry; contamination-resistant template-generated items; calibration and matched-item statistical comparison; and a published, reliability-measured error taxonomy. That combination is the contribution of LLM-STEMBench.
